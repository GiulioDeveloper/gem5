Top-Down Guide

What each top-down number in this project is called, exactly how the O3 core computes it, why it is computed that way, and where it can mislead you. The reference is Yasin's 2014 paper; every difference from it is marked.

Status

The accounting is under review. This guide describes the code with the 2026-11-13 front-end fix. Before it, front-end bound absorbed some back-end stalls: every run made before that date reports front-end bound too high (by at most 0.076 of slots at the median of the agentic base sweep) and back-end bound too low by the same amount. Several back-end nodes differ from the paper and are unchanged; see Known problems. Treat Level 2/3 of the back end as provisional.

The counters do not change simulated timing: every other statistic is identical with and without them.

The paper uses Intel's words; gem5 uses the same words for different pipeline points. This project follows gem5 in the code and on every wiki page.

So the paper's "issue-pipeline slots" are our rename slots, and its "uops executed in a cycle" are our micro-ops issued in a cycle. In tdm.cc, "issued" always has the gem5 meaning.

Where things are counted

Level 1 is counted at rename, in slots. The back-end split is counted at the IQ, in cycles. Keeping these two apart explains most of the confusion about the numbers.

FETCH

6 wide

Nothing counted here. Its delays show up as bubbles at rename.

DECODE

6 wide

Nothing counted. Decode-time redirects cost front-end latency.

RENAME · SLOTS

8 wide

Total slots, renamed micro-ops, fetch bubbles, refill bubbles, squashing and serialize cycles.

IQ · CYCLES

issue 8 wide

Execution stalls, load stalls by depth, store stalls.

EXECUTE

events

Branch mispredict and memory-order violation counts.

COMMIT

8 wide

Committed micro-ops (retiring).

Level 1

Four shares of the slots that add up to 1. Three are measured; back-end bound is what is left.

slots = 8 × topDown.cycles committed = commitStats0.numOps retiring = committed / slots frontendBound = rename.fetchBubbles / slots badSpeculation = (rename.renamedInsts − committed + rename.refillBubbles + 8 × rename.status::Squashing) / slots backendBound = 1 − (retiring + frontendBound + badSpeculation)

Retiring

level1.retiring · td_retiring · paper: Retiring
What
Slots that held a micro-op which later committed. Equals committed micro-ops per cycle divided by 8.
Why
Same as the paper. Counting micro-ops on both sides (renamed and committed) makes their difference exactly the squashed work.
Pitfalls
100% is 8 micro-ops per cycle, which this model cannot reach with a 6-wide front end. Micro-ops are not instructions (about 1.07 to 1.16 per instruction on our workloads).

Front-end bound

level1.frontendBound · td_frontend · paper: Frontend Bound
What
Slots the back end could have taken but the front end left empty, outside the refill after a squash.
How
Each cycle rename runs: capacity = min(8, free ROB entries, free IQ entries); bubbles = capacity − micro-ops available. Outside the refill window the bubbles go to fetchBubbles. When rename is Blocked, Squashing or in SerializeStall, nothing is counted. Nothing is counted either in a cycle where rename stopped on an instruction that had no load-queue entry, no store-queue entry or no physical register, or that must serialize.
Why
The capacity cap is the paper's "while there is no backend-stall" condition: a slot the back end could not take is not the front end's fault. Rename is in order: once it stops on one instruction nothing behind it can be renamed, so the back end's capacity that cycle is the number renamed, and the front end delivered more than that. Only when rename took everything delivered is the capacity for the missing micro-ops a guess; the code assumes they would have fit up to the free ROB and IQ entries.
Pitfalls
fixed 2026-11-13 Until this date the stop on registers, the store queue or the load queue was not excluded: rename retried every cycle and the slots its skid buffer did not cover were counted here. On kernels that fill the SQ or the register file the fix moved up to 0.27 of slots to back-end bound (L2 and DRAM chases with independent adds, streaming stores); cycle counts did not change. Runs from before the fix carry the leak.
After a rename stall ends, rename idles until decode and fetch restart. Those cycles count as fetch latency (0.16 of slots on a store kernel); a real core would have micro-ops queued. Open.
The 6-wide front end can never fill 8 slots, so 2 slots of every full-delivery cycle land here.
Fetch waits on every L1I hit after a taken branch, which inflates this on all workloads.

Bad speculation

level1.badSpeculation · td_badspec · paper: Bad Speculation
What
Three parts: slots of micro-ops that were renamed and never committed; all 8 slots of each cycle rename spends squashing; and the refill bubbles (empty slots in the 5 cycles after a squash, plus squashed micro-ops that reach rename).
Why
The first two are the paper's formula. The refill came with PR #2392: its idea is that the unavoidable pipeline refill is part of the misprediction cost.
Pitfalls
The paper counts the fetch from the correct target as front end (Branch Resteers), so this node is larger than the paper's and front-end bound smaller, by up to 40 slots per squash.
Overlapping squashes restart one refill window; they do not add 5 cycles each.
Each squashed micro-op met at rename adds one refill bubble without using a slot, so a cycle can count more than 8.

Back-end bound

level1.backendBound · td_backend · paper: Backend Bound
What
Every slot not claimed by the other three: slots rename could not fill because the ROB or IQ was full, it was blocked by the stage after it, or it was waiting to serialize.
Why
Same as the paper (1 minus the rest).
Pitfalls
It is a remainder, so any over-count elsewhere shrinks it silently. Before 2026-11-13 the front-end leak was taken straight out of this node.

Front end

fetchLatency = rename.fetchLatencyBubbles / slots fetchBandwidth = (rename.fetchBubbles − rename.fetchLatencyBubbles) / slots branchResteers = rename.resteerBubbles / slots (part of fetchLatency)

Fetch latency and fetch bandwidth

frontend.fetchLatency, frontend.fetchBandwidth · td_fe_latency, td_fe_bandwidth
What
Latency: the front-end bubbles of cycles in which no micro-op was available to rename. Bandwidth: the bubbles of cycles in which some arrived, but fewer than the back end could take.
Why
The paper's split: latency is fetch starvation whatever its cause, bandwidth is under-delivery. The two add up to front-end bound by construction.
Pitfalls
The paper counts latency in cycles with at least a full width of bubbles; here it is the bubbles of zero-delivery cycles, which is smaller when the back end could take fewer than 8.
Latency is not "instruction cache misses": on SPEC with almost no L1I misses fetch still waits on the L1I in 22 to 31% of cycles.
Bandwidth contains the 6-into-8 structural part and the register/SQ leak.

Branch resteers

frontend.branchResteers · td_fe_resteer
What
The latency bubbles between the end of a squash's refill and the first micro-op to reach rename afterwards.
Why
It shows how much longer than the 5-cycle minimum the front end takes to deliver the correct path.
Pitfalls
The paper's Branch Resteers is the whole delay after a flush. Ours is only the part beyond the refill; the refill itself is in bad speculation. The viewers do not stack it.

Bad speculation

branchMispredicts = badSpeculation × iew.branchMispredicts / (iew.branchMispredicts + iew.memOrderViolationEvents) machineClears = badSpeculation − branchMispredicts

Branch mispredicts and machine clears

badSpec.branchMispredicts, badSpec.machineClears · td_bs_branch, td_bs_clears
What
Bad speculation shared out in proportion to two event counts. Nothing is measured per slot.
Why
The paper's method: a ratio of event counts applied to the parent.
Pitfalls
Mispredicts are counted when a branch resolves at execute, including branches on a wrong path that an older squash later removes. The paper counts retired mispredicts.
The violation counter also counts violations that cause no squash because the core is already squashing.
Squashes started by commit (traps, interrupts, system calls, squash-after instructions) have no event, so their slots are shared between the two by proportion. Agentic windows have many of these.
Decode-time redirects are not in either: they never reach rename and cost front-end latency.

Back end

This is where the code differs most from the paper. The code first, then the paper.

The cycle counters (at the IQ, every cycle)

if ROB empty or issued > 2: nothing else: execStallCycles += 1 if issued == 0: if a load is outstanding: loadStallCycles += 1 deepest depth ≥ 1: loadStallL1Miss += 1 deepest depth ≥ 2: loadStallL2Miss += 1 else if store queue full: storeStallCycles += 1

"Deepest" is the largest miss depth over all outstanding loads in the load queue, not only the oldest.

As coded

serializeBound = 8 × rename.status::SerializeStall / slots rest = backendBound − serializeBound memoryBound = rest × (loadStallCycles + storeStallCycles) / execStallCycles coreBound = rest − memoryBound

In the paper (Table 2)

ExecutionStalls = cycles with few uops executed / Clocks Memory Bound = (MemStalls.AnyLoad + MemStalls.Stores) / Clocks Core Bound = ExecutionStalls − Memory Bound

The paper's Memory and Core are fractions of cycles measured at execute. They add up to ExecutionStalls, not to Backend Bound, and the paper says only sibling nodes are comparable. The code instead scales the same ratio into the back-end slots so the children add up to the parent. The memory-to-core ratio is identical; the absolute values are not.

Serialize

backend.serializeBound · td_be_serialize · not in the paper
What
All 8 slots of each cycle rename waits for the ROB to drain before an instruction that must run alone.
Why
It came with PR #2392. It is measured in slots, so it is exact where the other two children are estimates.
Pitfalls
In this Arm model every system-register read (MRS) serializes, including thread-local-storage and timer reads that a real core likely does not drain for. It is large on agentic windows (about 0.08 of slots) and probably overstated.
The same cycles also count as execution stalls, so they dilute the memory/core ratio a second time.

Memory bound

backend.memoryBound · td_be_memory · paper: Memory Bound
What
The share of the remaining back-end slots given by: cycles with nothing issued and a load outstanding (or the store queue full), over all execution-stall cycles.
Why
The paper's heuristic: a miss only costs when the scheduler has nothing else ready. Work the core does under a miss is deliberately not charged to memory.
Pitfalls
It is a lower bound. A cycle that issues 1 or 2 micro-ops with a miss outstanding is a core stall. A pointer chase through the L2 with 32 independent adds per iteration takes exactly as long as the bare chase, yet reports memory 0.08 and core 0.54.
A load waiting on a page-table walk is at depth 0, so walks count as L1 bound even when the walk goes to DRAM.
The ratio comes from all cycles, not only cycles where rename was back-end stalled.

Core bound

backend.coreBound · td_be_core · paper: Core Bound
What
The rest of the back-end slots: execution stalls that are not load or store stalls.
Why
Same construction as the paper (execution stalls minus memory).
Pitfalls
It is an upper bound, for the reason above: under a perfect L1D, core bound falls 63% on the agentic sweep.
The threshold of 2 was chosen for 4-wide cores; on an 8-wide issue stage 3 or 4 issued is still half idle but not counted.
The stall test is "ROB not empty"; Intel's is "scheduler not empty", which here would be the IQ. Cycles where the front end starves the core therefore count as core stalls.

Idle cycles

gem5 stops ticking a core when nothing is active, for example while the only thing in flight is a cache miss. Real hardware would still be clocked, so those cycles are added back when the core wakes.

  • Slots: if rename was running or idle when the core stopped, the idle cycles are front-end latency (those inside the refill window: bad speculation). If rename was blocked, they fall to back-end bound.
  • Cycle counters: the back end records its state when the core stops (ROB empty, deepest outstanding load, SQ full) and counts the idle cycles as stalls in that state.
  • subtractIdleCycles=True leaves idle cycles out of everything.
Pitfall. Idle cycles are 5 to 11% of cycles on agentic windows and nearly vanish with a perfect L1I. They are mostly instruction-miss waits, not an idle operating system.

Reading rules

  • Sum counters, then divide. Never average per-block fractions. Add the raw counters over the stats blocks of a window and apply the formulas once.
  • Compare siblings only. Fetch latency against fetch bandwidth, memory against core. A Level 2 value means little unless its parent is large.
  • Level 1 does not separate agentic from SPEC int on the windows read so far (front-end bound around half for both). The difference is in the make-up.
  • Use the limit studies for cause. Top-down says where slots are lost in this run. What a fix would gain comes from the perfect L1I, L1D, L2 and TLB runs.
  • Model effects to keep in mind: 6-wide fetch and decode into 8-wide rename; fetch waiting on L1I hits; serialization on every system-register read.

Under discussion

  • Done 2026-11-13: the front-end leak is fixed, by not counting front-end bubbles in a cycle where rename stopped for a back-end reason.
  • Decided 2026-11-13: the back-end split stays as it is for now; a redesign may come later.
  • Agreed, not implemented: follow the paper in not forcing the back-end children to add up to back-end bound.
  • Open: whether to replace the "few issued" test. The candidate attributes each back-end stall of rename to the oldest entry of the resource that stalled it (ROB head for the ROB, registers and LQ; oldest unissued micro-op for the IQ) and classifies what that entry waits on. It still cannot separate two bottlenecks that overlap.
  • Open: whether the restart lag after a rename stall should count as back-end bound.
  • Open: whether to keep the serialize node, and whether the refill belongs in bad speculation.
